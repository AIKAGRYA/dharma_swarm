We paved over a hole while writing about hole-paving; a reader doing what this essay tells readers to do would have caught us. On the morning of Friday, June 19, 2026, the Bürgenstock resort above Lake Lucerne stood ready to host the ceremony sealing a peace. The Swiss Federal Council had approved an army assistance mission and closed the airspace; Swiss press reported planning for as many as two thousand soldiers and police. Two days earlier the United States and Iran had signed — electronically, at a distance — a fourteen-point memorandum of understanding meant to end a war that had killed, among many others, the Supreme Leader of Iran. Friday was to mark that signing, already accomplished, and open the talks that would implement it.

The ceremony did not happen. The Swiss ministry's public ticker recorded the event under a heading that said postponed, not cancelled, in four sentences: "The planned talks between the US, Iran, Qatar and Pakistan have been postponed. Switzerland remains ready to facilitate these talks. The relevant preparatory work at Bürgenstock is continuing. No further information can be provided at present."

No explanation was ever provided. The American vice president suggested logistical difficulties on the Iranian side. The Associated Press, citing Al Mayadeen — a broadcaster close to Iran and Hezbollah — floated the theory that Tehran was protesting continued Israeli strikes on Lebanon; the Swiss public broadcaster SRF carried that theory under a one-word label, *unbestätigt*: unconfirmed. Three weeks and two bombing campaigns later, no government, journalist, or leak has established why June 19 collapsed. The hole has edges — we map two of them below — but it has no floor.

Hold on to that. It is the shape of the entire story. The summer's most consequential sequence — a superpower and a decapitated regional power signing a peace, breaking it, bombing each other through a state funeral, then circling back toward talks — is riddled with holes like that one, sitting at the load-bearing points: what standing the peace's only public text has, who fired the shots that ended the lull, why the diplomacy stalled. Hour by hour, the discourse fills each with confident narrative. This essay will not fill the holes; it will map them — on this subject, perhaps the only honest service available. The correction above is the proof of how hard that is: the mappers fell in once already.

## The rules of this piece

Everything below belongs to one of three registers, kept visibly apart. **Verified**: a dated document or event anchored to a primary source or multiple independent outlets. **Claimed**: a statement by a government, a military, or an anonymous official about something we cannot check — including every statement a party makes about its own actions. When US Central Command announces it struck "more than 80 targets," that is CENTCOM's account of CENTCOM; when Iran's Health Ministry counts its dead, that is Tehran's account of Tehran. Both may be true; neither has been independently verified, and in this war almost nothing has. **Unknown** means unknown — not "unknown, but here is what insiders whisper."

One more rule, the one this publication exists to keep: we will state the strongest version of each side's case before doubting it, and say at the end what would change our reading.

## The spine

What can actually be established runs as follows.

The war began on February 28, 2026, with US and Israeli strikes on Iran — the fighting, in Reuters' words, "the U.S. and Israel started on February 28." Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, eighty-six, was killed that day. On March 8 the Assembly of Experts named his son, Mojtaba Khamenei, as successor; he has not been seen in public since. After February 28 the IAEA stopped all verification work in Iran; its one documented return, per the Institute for Science and International Security, was a June 1–3 visit to the civilian power plant at Bushehr.

In mid-June, after an April ceasefire had frayed and a violent first week of June, a deal came together with startling speed. Trump announced it on Sunday, June 14, saying he had ordered a naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted; on Monday he said the pact was "all signed." It was not: the Swiss foreign ministry and Al Jazeera both date the signature — electronic, at a distance — to Wednesday, June 17. Per Al Jazeera: "Neither has released a physical copy, but a US official read out the text during a call with reporters," and Iranian officials had "yet to confirm the US version of the text." The read-out did not evaporate on the call: the same day, Axios published a full fourteen-point transcript of it, under the outlet's own caveat that "because the official read the deal to reporters and the print text has not been released, this transcript may be inexact." That transcript is, to this day, the only full text of this peace the public has seen. The next morning the Swiss ministry's ticker welcomed the signing; its June 18 entry already welcomed "the agreement on a roadmap aimed at reaching a final agreement within 60 days" — which is why SRF dates the sixty-day clock from June 18, expiring around August 17.

Then the Bürgenstock ceremony collapsed on June 19 — and, remarkably, the summit happened anyway. On June 21–22 the resort hosted what the mediators named the Lake Lucerne Summit: Vance leading the US side, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi for Iran, Pakistani and Qatari mediation between them. The talks ran through the night. The mediators' joint statement recorded "encouraging progress… including the creation of a mechanism for further technical talks" and that "the High Level Committee has agreed upon a roadmap towards reaching a final deal within 60 days." The Swiss ticker, the most reliable dated record of this diplomacy, goes silent after June 22. It has not spoken since.

The rest is breakdown, in two waves. On June 25 a drone struck the cargo ship *Ever Lovely* in the strait; the US blamed Iran, and on the night of June 26 US forces struck Iranian military sites — the first American strikes after the signed memorandum, Vance posting that "violence will be met with violence." Iran hit US facilities in Kuwait and Bahrain; a Qatari national aboard a missing vessel died of shrapnel wounds. By June 28, per a lone US official quoted by Reuters, both sides had agreed to stop and talk — and, for a while, they did. Tanker traffic through Hormuz rebounded: in the week before the July attacks, an average of thirty-three tankers a day crossed the strait, per Kpler ship-tracking data cited by CNBC. Technical talks convened in Doha on June 30 and July 1, addressing, per Iran's deputy foreign minister, American "violations of its obligations" and six billion dollars in frozen Iranian funds. Qatar announced the next meeting would come after the funeral of Ali Khamenei.

The funeral ran July 4 to 9 — Tehran, Qom, Najaf, Karbala, burial at the Imam Reza shrine in Mashhad. The son and successor was absent throughout.

During it, the war resumed. On the night of July 6–7, three commercial ships were attacked in or near the strait within twenty-four hours, one of them a Qatari-owned gas tanker — the mediator's own flag. On July 7 CENTCOM announced strikes on more than eighty targets; on July 8, at the NATO summit in Ankara, Trump said: "To me, I think it's over… I don't want to deal with them anymore… As far as I'm concerned, it's over." That night and into July 9 — the morning Khamenei was lowered into the ground at Mashhad — the US struck roughly ninety more targets across Iran, and Iran fired at US facilities in four Gulf and Levantine states. On July 10 Trump posted that "the Cease Fire is OVER!" — while saying, within the same days, that Tehran "wants to make a deal." Qatari mediators flew to Iran that Friday. As of this writing, July 12, an anonymous US official tells ABC that technical talks "continue." Neither side has formally left the memorandum. The sixty-day clock, for whatever it now binds, is still running.

That is the spine. Now the holes it hangs on.

## The war over a text only one side has vouched for

Here is what is public, stated precisely — because the first draft of this essay stated it wrong. Since June 17, anyone can read the memorandum, in the limited sense that matters least: Axios published a full fourteen-point transcript of the US official's read-out the day of the signing, flagging on its own authority that "the print text has not been released" and the transcript "may be inexact." What no one can read is an instrument. No signed copy has been released — not by Washington, not by Tehran, not by the mediators — and Iran has never confirmed the version it was read from. Per Al Jazeera: "Neither has released a physical copy, but a US official read out the text during a call with reporters." The sole full public text of this war's peace is one outlet's transcript of one belligerent's reading, vouched for by no second party. Even the document's name is unstable: the Qatar–Pakistan statement calls it the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, after talks held in April.

Since July 7, both governments have accused each other of violating specific articles of this text. Iran conditioned June technical talks on Articles 1, 10 and 11 — an end to fighting including in Lebanon, sanctions relief, frozen assets. In July, Ghalibaf posted over his own name a list headed "Major MOU Violations by the US," opening with "Violating Iranian adjustments in the Strait" and including "Reinstating oil sanctions." The US calls Iranian ship attacks a "gross violation" of the same document. Even the mediators adjudicate by clause number in public: the Qatar–Pakistan statement cites "paragraph 5 of the MoU" on safe passage through Hormuz.

And here the published text starts to work. Paragraph 5, in the Axios transcript, has Iran making "arrangements using its best efforts for the safe passage of commercial vessels" and opening a dialogue with Oman "to define the future administration and maritime services in the Strait of Hormuz" — recognizably the arrangement Ghalibaf's phrase "Iranian adjustments in the Strait" asserts. Paragraph 13 makes final-deal negotiations conditional on implementing "paragraphs 1, 4, 5, 10, and 11" — a list that contains exactly the articles Iran cited in Doha. The BBC's description of the oil waiver matches paragraph 10. The transcript keeps passing consistency checks against what the parties and the mediators say in public. Consistency is evidence that it is genuine. It is not authentication — and neither government has provided that.

So the discipline this section urged in its first, wrong draft survives, sharpened. When a commentator says the strikes were justified because Iran breached the deal, or that the strikes were the breach, the question is no longer only *which clause?* It is: **which clause, in whose text, vouched for by whom?** A reader can now look up what paragraph 10 purportedly says; what no reader can do is confirm that paragraph 10 as transcribed is paragraph 10 as signed. Every violation claim in this war is being adjudicated, in public, against a text with one voucher — and the discourse has treated its standing as settled without once asking who stands behind it. The hole at the center is thinner than we first drew it. It is still the center.

## Three ships, one attribution

The proximate cause of the July bombing — what Trump called "retribution" — is the attack on three commercial vessels on July 6–7. Who attacked them?

The public record answers: *the United States says Iran did.* Axios's own headline carried the load-bearing clause — "Iran attacks three ships in 24 hours, U.S. says." CNN's report rests on "a US official" — anonymous — who called the strikes a "gross violation" of the memorandum. Iran never formally claimed the attacks. Iranian state television confirmed that a tanker carrying Qatari gas came under attack "after ignoring warnings" — a sentence that justifies without confessing.

The primary maritime record, meanwhile, attributes nothing. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations said it had received a report from a tanker struck by "an unknown projectile" — a sentence that sits inside the same Axios article whose headline carries the American attribution. The one non-belligerent entry in the public record declines to name a shooter.

The attribution is plausible — the implicit justification on state TV, the pattern of prior Iranian attacks in June, the absence of a competing suspect in Iran's home strait all point one way. But *plausible* is not *established*: no independent forensic attribution has been published, and the one primary maritime report in the record declines to attribute at all. The casus belli for a two-night, 170-target air campaign rests, in public, on the say-so of the belligerent that carried it out, part of it anonymous. In a healthier information ecology that sentence would be scandalous. In this one, it is Tuesday.

The same asymmetry runs through every number in the July record: the only casualty figures — at least fourteen dead, seventy-eight injured — are Iran's Health Ministry's, counted by no one else, and the Revolutionary Guard's claim of eighty-five US military sites struck is verified by no one. Two governments are the sole narrators of their own war, and nearly every referee has been kept off the field: no IAEA inspector is documented inside a bombed nuclear site since February, and Ghalibaf, on July 2, denied the agency had access to Fordow, Natanz or Isfahan at all.

## The funeral week

One sequence is fully sourced, and deserves plain statement, because most analysis slides past it.

Qatar — the co-mediator — had said publicly that the next meeting would come after the funeral processions, which ran July 4 to 9. On July 7, in the middle of them, the US Treasury revoked the sanctions waiver on Iranian crude-oil sales — a waiver that, per the BBC, "was part of the memorandum of understanding" — and did so *before* the first strike package was announced that day.

The published text makes that revocation checkable. Paragraph 10 of the Axios transcript has the United States undertaking that "immediately upon the signing of this MOU and until the termination of sanctions, the U.S. Department of Treasury will issue waivers for the export of Iranian crude oil, petroleum products, and derivatives." On either reading of the waiver's term — Axios's own summary glosses it as covering the sixty days; the paragraph's words run "until the termination of sanctions" — July 7 falls inside the window. If the transcript is the signed text, the revocation-before-the-strikes is a prima facie breach of the deal's plain words by the United States, checkable by any reader — the strongest text-grounded breach claim either side can currently make in public. It carries its source's standing, no more: one outlet's transcript of one side's reading. But Ghalibaf's list said exactly this — "Reinstating oil sanctions" — and for once, a belligerent's claim can be laid against a text.

The two heaviest nights of American bombing coincided with the movement of the dead Supreme Leader's body toward its burial at Mashhad. The man being buried had been killed by American and Israeli bombs on the war's first day; his son and successor, reported months ago by American officials to have been wounded in the same strikes, was absent from every ceremony — and who in Tehran actually authorized the July retaliation is unknown.

We do not know what the ship attacks were: a Revolutionary Guard faction testing the new leadership's control, authorized enforcement of whatever Hormuz arrangement Iran believes the memorandum grants it, or something else. What the record supports is narrower and colder: **the diplomatic track's own mediator had scheduled peace for after the mourning, and the war did not wait for it.**

## The two stories, told at full strength

The American case, steelmanned: Iran attacked civilian shipping — repeatedly — in the most important energy chokepoint on earth, and a ceasefire that does not protect neutral tankers protects nothing. The US response targeted radar, fast boats, missile storage — the tools of those attacks — and its stated aim, to "degrade" Iran's ability "to threaten freedom of navigation," matches the target list — and, in the published text, matches paragraph 5, where Iran undertakes "arrangements using its best efforts for the safe passage of commercial vessels." Washington kept the talks channel open even while striking: the strikes, in this reading, exist to make the memorandum's shipping provisions real.

The Iranian case, steelmanned: the memorandum's core bargain — an end to fighting including in Lebanon, sanctions relief, unfrozen assets — was, by Tehran's account in Doha, not being implemented. The US revoked the oil waiver — in the published text, a paragraph-10 undertaking running "until the termination of sanctions" — before the July strikes rather than after any adjudication. Ghalibaf's "Iranian adjustments in the Strait" asserts that Iran's conduct in Hormuz was within the arrangement, not a breach of it. American bombs fell during the state funeral of a leader America killed, struck near the country's only civilian nuclear power plant, and reached Tehran — while the American president called Iranians "scum" and mused about seizing Kharg Island. Retaliation, in this reading, was the minimum a sovereign could do and survive.

Notice what has changed now that a text is public, even a one-sided one: the two cases are no longer symmetric, and honesty requires saying so. Iran's strongest plank is now text-checkable and prima facie strong — paragraph 10 promises waivers, and the Treasury revoked mid-term, before the strikes. Washington's strongest plank rests on shipping commitments that verifiably exist in the published text and in the mediators' own citations — but its factual half hangs on an attribution that remains anonymous and unproven. One case can point to a clause; the other must first prove a shooter. That asymmetry is a finding, not a verdict: the text has one voucher, and the attribution may yet prove out. What remains engineered is the fog itself — thinner and truer than this essay first drew it: Tehran will not confirm the text; Washington will not release a signed copy. Between those two refusals, most observers' conclusions still reduce to prior loyalty — but the discourse could now check more than it does, and mostly declines to.

## The ecology of the holes

Watch the system around a hole. On June 21, Al Jazeera reported an Iranian walkout mid-summit, triggered by Trump threats, complete with a bristling Ghalibaf quote. No other outlet confirmed it; the Swiss ticker records only arrivals, meetings, progress. Perhaps it happened; perhaps it was theater described to a reporter. Either way the story ran, was aggregated, entered the record — singular sourcing and all.

Or watch the edges of the June 19 hole itself — both sitting in this essay's own sources, both missed by its first draft. Al Jazeera's summit report states flatly, attributed to no one, that the Friday talks were postponed "after Israel launched deadly attacks in Lebanon following the deaths of four of its soldiers in combat" — a causal explanation wearing the grammar of fact, and one that, if true, would locate the trigger inside the very matter the published text's first paragraph covers: "the immediate and permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon." And a named source marks the hole's near edge: on Thursday, June 18 — the day before the postponement — Pakistan's prime minister cancelled his trip to the ceremony, his spokesman Mosharraf Zaidi stating: "The proposed visit has been postponed as the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding has already been electronically signed, has entered into force, and is now under implementation." The mediator's principal publicly withdrew a day early, with a stated reason that treats the ceremony as superfluous. Neither datum explains the collapse; both are edges. Mapping a hole means mapping its edges — and the mappers, as this essay's correction records, are inside the machinery too.

Or watch a hole get filled with the wrong war entirely. High in July 2026 search results for this conflict sit reports that Iran's parliament voted to close the Strait of Hormuz after US strikes on nuclear sites — stories from The Hill and Anadolu describing events of **June 2025**, the previous war, one wearing a trending stamp dated 07.09.26. A reader, an aggregation bot, or a chatbot summarizing the news would fold last year's escalation into this year's seamlessly. Some of the confident discourse about this month's war is, verifiably, about a different one.

This is the real subject. War produces fog; that is old. New is the machinery that instantly manufactures certainty from it — the anonymous-official pipeline that lets governments narrate deniably, the headline grammar that buries "U.S. says" behind the comma, the aggregation layer that strips even that, and audiences trained by feeds to experience "developing" as "known." The hole does not stay open long enough to be felt. It is filled within the hour, by whatever travels fastest — never the sentence "no further information can be provided at present." The machinery does not exempt its critics: the correction at the head of this piece records the hour this essay filled a hole with confidence of its own.

Switzerland is the control group: the one actor whose every public sentence was checkable — dated ticker entries, no anonymous color, its role scrupulously "facilitator," never mediator — and the actor the coverage found least useful. Its record simply stops on June 22, the honest silence of an institution with nothing verified to add. In the present information economy, the reward for speaking only when you can stand behind it is that the conversation routes around you.

## What would change this picture

Corrections are not a concession of this method; they are its product — this essay already carries one. Our reading would move if any of the following surfaced: release of a signed instrument, or authentication of the published transcript, by any signatory or mediator; independent forensic attribution of the July 6–7 ship attacks — a flag-state investigation, an insurer's report, imagery analysis from a party not at war; any IAEA account of access to Fordow, Natanz, Isfahan, or the strikes near Bushehr; a named-source account of why June 19 collapsed; casualty verification from any body not run by a belligerent. If a signed text surfaces and differs from the Axios transcript, we will say where, and what the difference does to each side's claims — whichever side it favors.

## Why this is the story

It would be easy to close by predicting. The prediction industry around this conflict is fully staffed; its most defensible entry — the BBC's Jeremy Bowen: "For all his bluster, Trump has no better option than talks with Iran" — is a judgment about options, not outcomes.

We end somewhere else. Between February and July, a Supreme Leader was killed and buried, his successor vanished from view, a peace was signed whose only public text is one outlet's transcript of one government's reading — never confirmed by the other — a Swiss mountain prepared for a ceremony that dissolved without explanation, and two air campaigns were launched on attributions the public cannot check. Every clause in that sentence is verified, and together they describe not just a war but the condition under which the war is known: a handful of dated facts, load-bearing holes at every joint, and a discourse that cannot tolerate a hole for an hour.

The disciplined response is not cynicism — the verified spine of this essay exists because reporters in the ledger below did careful work under fire. The disciplined response is register: holding *verified*, *claimed*, and *unknown* apart with enough stubbornness that the unknowns stay visible, because the unknowns are where the next escalation will be justified. Nobody outside two governments knows why June 19 collapsed, whether the text the public reads is the text the belligerents signed, or who fired at the ships that restarted the bombing. Nobody knows why — and until that changes, that is the story. Saying so is not a failure of journalism. It is the part of journalism the feed deleted.

What we see, we will say. What we cannot see — we will say that too.

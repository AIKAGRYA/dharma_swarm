In the summer of 2026, Senator Marsha Blackburn, campaigning for governor of Tennessee, released an advertisement warning of "Chinese front companies" moving on the state's farmland. "China," she promised, "won't be able to get their hands on one blade of grass in Tennessee." The Nashville Banner, examining the ad, added the detail the ad had not: the data provide no known examples of Chinese-owned farms in Tennessee.

A defense of ground no one is buying. That is the panic in its purest form. But the question under the ad deserves a serious answer, because the serious answer is stranger than either side of the argument: the people selling the fear do not have the numbers they claim, and neither do the people selling the reassurance. The ledger both camps preach from is broken, and the brokenness is the real story.

## The ledger

Since 1978, a law called AFIDA has required every foreign person acquiring an interest in American agricultural land to file a disclosure with the Department of Agriculture. The latest annual report — published January 2026, covering holdings through December 31, 2024 — is the primary document in this debate. We read it directly rather than reading about it.

What it says: foreign persons of every nationality combined held "an interest in nearly 46 million acres of U.S. agricultural land" — in the report's own words, "3.6 percent of all privately held agricultural land and 2 percent of all land in the United States." Of that, Chinese-linked filers reported 247,659 acres. USDA's narrative calls this "slightly less than 1 percent" of the foreign-held total; its own tables say 0.54 percent. Note what just happened: the flagship narrative of the very report this fight is fought over misstates its own headline ratio by nearly a factor of two, in the same direction of sloppiness its auditor had already documented. And against the whole estate — roughly 1.3 billion privately held agricultural acres — the Chinese share is about 0.02 percent: one acre in every five thousand.

Who holds that acre? The report is specific. Five companies account for 92 percent of everything: Brazos Highland Properties (86,994 acres — the Val Verde County, Texas ranch-and-wind holding linked to the private billionaire Sun Guangxin), two subsidiaries of Smithfield Foods (about 100,000 acres combined — the pork producer bought by Hong Kong-listed WH Group in a 2013 acquisition cleared by the federal government), Harvest Texas (29,705), and U.S. Agri-Chemicals (11,263). The remainder scatters across dozens of small direct registrants; the newest filings include vineyards, ten-acre parcels, and a South Dakota couple holding 9.95 acres.

Then the sentence that should reframe the conversation, quoted exactly: **"There were no filings directly by the government of China."** Iranian filers reported 547 acres; Russian investors, 11; North Korea, none.

For scale: Canadian investors hold 16.1 million acres — sixty-five times the Chinese figure. The Netherlands holds roughly ten percent of all foreign acreage; Germany, Italy, and the United Kingdom about six percent each. None of these has generated a statute.

The trajectory runs opposite to the rhetoric. Chinese-linked acreage peaked at 383,935 acres in 2021 and has fallen by roughly a third since. New Chinese-linked acquisitions reported in all of 2024: eight filings totaling about 3,283 acres, the largest a 1,513-acre parcel in Kings County, California. A further softness sits inside these numbers: AFIDA counts leases of ten years or longer as foreign "holdings," and the report notes that in Texas — which leads all states with 123,708 Chinese-linked acres — "long-term leases associated with wind energy investment predominate" — and that a turbine's footprint "leaves most of the parcel available for agricultural use." A meaningful share of "Chinese-owned Texas land" is a wind lease on a ranch the rancher still works; the deflation is the government's own sentence, not ours.

## The broken book

If the story ended there, this would be a debunking, and debunkings are cheap. It does not, because in January 2024 the Government Accountability Office examined how those numbers are made, and what it found cuts against everyone.

USDA, the GAO reported, "does not sufficiently verify and conduct quality reviews to track the accuracy and completeness of its collected AFIDA data." The filings arrived on paper, keyed in by hand. The largest China-associated holding in the country had been counted twice. The Defense Department told GAO it needed data more current, specific, and frequent than a once-a-year report; CFIUS, the federal committee that reviews foreign investment for security risk, was not regularly receiving the data at all. The federal government literally did not have a reliable count of the thing the country was panicking about — and the repair, as we will see, is only half-begun.

The unreliability runs in both directions, and honesty requires saying so.

In the panic's favor: USDA's own report states that its China figure "should be interpreted as a minimum," because multi-country ownership structures fall out of the country tallies. And there is a concrete anomaly. Tianqiao Chen, a Chinese-born billionaire, holds nearly 200,000 acres of Oregon timberland — "the second largest foreign owner of U.S. land, according to land ownership publication The Land Report," as Oregon Public Broadcasting put it in 2024. Oregon does not appear among the five states that hold 92 percent of the AFIDA China ledger. His holding — which by itself would nearly double the official China total — cannot be inside it. That impossibility is not a hypothesis; it is arithmetic on the report's own tables — the top five states account for all but roughly twenty thousand of the counted acres, and Oregon is not among them. Where is it? The American Farm Bureau, working from USDA's data, attributes part of the post-2021 decline to USDA reclassifying acreage "originally attributed to Chinese investors but later confirmed to be owned by a U.S. land asset management company with operations in Chinese markets." The obvious inference we state as the hypothesis it is: that Chen's timber empire is that reclassified company, and that part of the celebrated one-third decline is bookkeeping rather than divestment. The test is concrete and public: pull USDA's underlying data files and check the country code on the entity that bought the Oregon land. We have not run it. Until someone does, "China is buying up our farmland" and "Chinese holdings are collapsing" are both stories told from a ledger that cannot support either. Though not symmetrically: on the ledger's own arithmetic, the panic story needs something like a hundredfold correction to matter — every acre of Chen's timber added to the count still yields about 0.035 percent of privately held agricultural land — while the reassurance needs only a rough doubling to fail.

In the reassurance's favor: the government's own headline numbers disagree with each other. In July 2025, announcing a National Farm Security Action Plan, Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins said "more than 265,000 acres in the United States are owned by Chinese nationals," with 162,167 in Texas, 14,382 in Virginia, and 32,447 in Utah. Six months later her own department's report said 247,659; Texas 123,708; Virginia 4,654; Utah absent from the top five. Neither matches the department's report covering 2023, which we also fetched: 277,336 acres; Texas 123,708; Utah 33,035; Virginia absent from its top five. The Secretary's numbers match neither the ledger before her statement nor the one after it. The instability is itself the finding.

Enforcement, meanwhile, has been homeopathic relative to the rhetoric. Total AFIDA penalties assessed in 2025, against all countries: $245,357 — with the largest single penalty going to a Canadian data-center company.

## The docket

Strip away the campaign ads and the documented national-security record consists of three cases, worth knowing precisely because each shows the system doing something different.

**Grand Forks.** In 2022, Fufeng Group, a Chinese company, moved to build a corn-milling plant on 300 acres roughly twelve miles from Grand Forks Air Force Base in North Dakota. CFIUS examined the deal and found it had *no jurisdiction* — a greenfield project, and the base was not then on the list of protected installations. That was a gap, not a clearance. An assistant secretary of the Air Force then wrote that the project "presents a significant threat to national security with both near- and long-term risks" — an official assertion we attribute rather than adopt, because the trade press that carried the letter noted that it "didn't offer any evidence that Fufeng is a threat, nor did it detail what kind of risks the company's business might pose," and one China-security expert pointed out that a Chinese-owned aircraft plant was already operating in the area. Whether a classified basis existed is unknowable from open sources; we hold it open. The city council killed the project's permits, 5–0. And then the system repaired itself: a Treasury rule effective December 9, 2024 added dozens of installations to CFIUS's real-estate jurisdiction — including Grand Forks Air Force Base, now inside a 100-mile review radius. The one proven failure produced a concrete federal fix. That is what functioning policy looks like: a specific parcel, a specific base, a specific rule.

**Arkansas.** The one case where "Chinese government-owned" is literally accurate: a seed company ultimately owned by ChemChina, a genuine state-owned enterprise, held about 160 acres in Craighead County. In October 2023 the state attorney general ordered divestment within two years and collected a $280,000 penalty, the statutory maximum. Existing law, an attorney general, done.

**Val Verde County.** The largest China-linked holding in America — Brazos Highland's 86,994 acres near Laughlin Air Force Base — was fined $120,216 as an AFIDA *non-filer*; Texas Public Radio reported in 2020 that a CFIUS analysis found the associated wind project "does not currently pose a national security concern." Claims of surveillance intent are op-ed assertions, not documented findings.

One jurisdictional gap, fixed. One state-owned seed plot, divested. One billionaire's wind ranch, reviewed and fined for paperwork. That is the entire documented docket beneath a five-year legislative wave.

## What the laws actually do

The wave is real. Since 2021, 525 bills have been introduced in the states and Congress, and 63 percent of them restrict Chinese citizens specifically, by the count of the Committee of 100, a Chinese-American civic organization that opposes them; the National Agricultural Law Center counted 28 states with restrictions as of October 2025. Texas's SB 17, read in the enrolled statute, is the fullest expression: it reaches agricultural land, commercial and industrial property, residences, groundwater, minerals, timber, and water rights; it designates target countries by pointing at the intelligence community's annual threat assessment (currently China, Iran, North Korea, Russia) and lets the governor add more; penalties run to half a property's market value, and a knowing purchase by an individual is a felony. Florida's law bars any person "domiciled in the People's Republic of China" who is not a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident from acquiring real property in the state, with one narrow carve-out — a single residence, up to two acres, at least five miles from any military installation, for a buyer legally present on a valid visa; a federal appeals court let it stand in November 2025, on the threshold ground that the plaintiffs lacked standing — indeed, the court read the Chinese plaintiffs, domiciled in Florida rather than China, as outside the law's scope altogether.

Now watch who these statutes bind, and who they exempt. The Chinese state filed for zero acres; the working weight falls on individuals — the China-domiciled, visa-holders — the bottom categories of a four-way collapse the discourse performs constantly, fusing the Chinese state, Chinese companies, Chinese nationals, and Chinese immigrants into a single word. Both statutes exempt citizens and green-card holders; Florida's law reaches the China-domiciled person who is neither, who may at most buy one small house far from a base. The line is still drawn around persons rather than conduct — nationality-adjacent by design, and upheld so far only on standing.

And the largest actual Chinese-owned agribusiness walks through the wall built in its name. Oklahoma banned Chinese-owned farmland in 2023 — then amended the ban in 2024 to exempt companies with federal CFIUS agreements, which shielded precisely Smithfield's roughly 2,575 Oklahoma acres; Smithfield spent $1.58 million lobbying the state across 2024–25, by Investigate Midwest's accounting. The law center's sober summary of the whole wave, late 2025: "actual enforcement activity remains limited."

The steelman, because the restrictionists are not all cynics: the Fufeng gap was real; the Pentagon itself asked for better, faster data; USDA's "minimum" caveat means honest hawks are right that the true footprint could exceed the count; some advocates argue sincerely from Beijing's stated doctrine of civil-military fusion. The strongest form of the argument is prospective, not retrospective — restriction as pre-positioning against state-directed acquisition that has not yet happened, on the theory that by the time a documented docket exists it is too late. And the serious version of the Smithfield worry was never its acreage but its position in the pork supply chain: concentration, not soil. The critique is not that concern is illegitimate — it is that the laws the concern produced do not track it. They miss the state that never filed, exempt the company that lobbied, and land on the couple with ten acres.

## Who profits

Follow the incentives and the panic resolves into documented constituencies.

First, politicians of both parties: the issue polls, costs nothing, and loses no farm votes — Blackburn's blade of grass only the most recent entry. Second, an organized advocacy layer: Responsible Statecraft — an outlet of the Quincy Institute, itself a combatant in this fight, so weigh accordingly — reported in June 2026 that State Armor, the group pushing model legislation state by state, was founded in 2023 by an alumnus of the Cicero Institute (co-founded by Palantir's Joe Lonsdale) and received $3.3 million from the Leonard Leo-linked Concord Fund; the same reporting notes Nebraska's registration law has produced zero registrants. Third, incumbent competitors: the same Responsible Statecraft reporting notes that Nebraska Governor Jim Pillen's family business, Pillen Family Farms, "is a direct competitor to Smithfield." Fourth, the sellers: the National Association of Realtors — a trade body with an interest in foreign demand — counted Chinese buyers as the top foreign purchasers of American homes in the year through March 2025, $13.7 billion worth, at the highest average prices of any nationality.

And then the tell. Ten months after the administration branded Chinese farmland ownership a national-security emergency, the president — in Beijing, with a reported $17-billion-a-year agricultural purchase agreement on the table — told an interviewer that pushing Chinese money out of American farmland would make farmers "lose a lot of money": "You want to see farm prices drop... Just take that out of the market." A threat that can be traded away against soybean orders was being priced, not feared.

## What a clear-eyed policy would watch

Four things, none of which require a single new speech.

**The map.** Secretary Rollins claimed much Chinese-owned land sits near critical bases. No methodology or geocoded dataset accompanied the claim — and GAO's central finding was that the government could not run that analysis. Publish the overlay: foreign parcels against installations, every nationality. If the clustering is real, show it.

**The beneficial owner.** The Chen anomaly shows the ledger can move by hundreds of thousands of acres on a country-code edit. Track ultimate beneficial ownership, or the numbers will keep meaning nothing in either direction.

**Conduct, not passport.** When a Saudi-owned farm was draining Arizona groundwater to grow export alfalfa, the state ended it in 2023–24 with existing contract law — lease violations, not nationality (reported by CNN and others; not fetched in full this session). Groundwater extraction, siting near sensitive installations, failure to file: regulate the behavior, whoever does it — including the Canadians who hold sixty-five times the acreage.

**Finish the rebuild.** The real reform is unglamorous and half-done: an online AFIDA filing portal launched in January 2026; the full regulatory overhaul — electronic database, stronger enforcement — was still only a *proposed* rule as of June 25, 2026; the Agriculture Secretary sits on CFIUS only for certain transactions, not as a permanent member. The count is the whole game. Fund the count.

What would change our mind: a geocoded federal dataset showing systematic foreign-adversary clustering around installations; documented PRC-state-directed acquisition; or the Chen test coming back — in either direction.

## The ground

The reader who arrived afraid should leave seeing two things at once; both are true, and neither cancels the other. The ground beneath the panic is thin: one acre in five thousand, falling for four years, held mostly by two pork companies, a seed conglomerate, and one billionaire's wind ranch — and no filing, anywhere, by the government of China. The ground beneath the reassurance is unsurveyed: a ledger the government's own auditor called unverified, that double-counted its largest entry, that cannot say whether 200,000 acres of Oregon is in it at all, and whose keepers announce numbers that match neither the ledger before them nor the one after.

The blade of grass in the Tennessee ad was never in danger. The instrument built to watch it was broken the entire time, and is being repaired only now, quietly — a proposed rule, a web portal — while the shouting happens elsewhere. Fear is being sold by people who have not read the ledger, and comfort by people who have not checked whether the ledger is true. We decline to buy either. What we see, we will say. What we cannot see — that too.

On Friday, June 12, 2026, at 5:21 in the evening Washington time, the United States Department of Commerce sent a letter to Anthropic. The letter itself is unpublished; the order, as Anthropic described it and Lawfare reconstructed it, was to "suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States, including foreign national Anthropic employees." An API serving the world cannot verify the citizenship of its callers. So Anthropic did the only compliant thing available: it turned both models off, everywhere, for everyone.

A disclosure before anything else: this essay was drafted by a model belonging to the family named in that directive. We flag it once and proceed; every source here is external and independent, and gates — unlike minds — are checkable.

Two frontier models went dark globally, for nearly three weeks, on the strength of one Friday-evening letter. No rulemaking, no comment period, no court. The mechanism, per Lawfare, was an "is informed" letter under the Export Control Reform Act, riding the deemed-export doctrine — "at least a facially plausible legal framework," though whether a foreign national querying an American model over an API is an "export" at all is contested. The trigger, per The Record: an Amazon research report describing a jailbreak technique. The exit, June 30 and July 1, per The Record: a classifier blocking the technique in "more than 99% of cases," expanded pre-release access for government evaluators, Mythos restored only to vetted US organizations; and, per PIIE, international access to Fable restored "with more restrictive guardrails."

This is the most dramatic gate yet closed around a frontier model, and whether it was the shape of things to come or a one-off improvisation is this essay's question. To ask it honestly you must first sort the gates into piles — enacted, proposed, imagined — because arguments about "AI regulation" routinely treat all three as one.

## Three inks

Draw the map, as of July 2026.

**Enacted and operating.** Europe's general-purpose-AI obligations, in force since August 2025, with Commission fining powers — up to 3% of global turnover or €15 million — activating August 2, 2026. China's mandatory service registration and content-labeling regime. California's SB 53 (September 2025) and New York's RAISE Act (December 2025). A January 13, 2026 federal rule moving advanced-chip exports to China to case-by-case review. And the machinery of Executive Order 14365 — a Justice Department litigation task force aimed at state AI laws.

**Enacted but ad hoc and reversible.** The June letter. Imposed and mostly lifted inside three weeks.

**Proposed.** The White House's March 2026 "National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" — we read the PDF; it is recommendations to Congress and binds no one. A proposed FTC policy statement (July 7, 2026, comment open). A 269-page bipartisan House bill, per Cybersecurity Dive (not fetched; treat as reported). Senator Hawley's S.321, prohibiting AI import and export with China: per Congress.gov, introduced January 29, 2025 — latest action still January 29, 2025, zero cosponsors.

**Imagined.** The replacement export rule Commerce promised in May 2025 — fourteen months, nothing. The list of "onerous" state laws the executive order demanded within ninety days — still unpublished at last report. Any federal preemption statute.

Hold the map: nearly everything loud in the American conversation lives in the last two piles.

## The gates that opened

The deepest surprise in the record is not which gates closed. It is how many opened.

The Biden administration's "Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion," issued January 15, 2025, contained the only control ever placed on **model weights** themselves — ECCN 4E091, covering closed-weight models trained above 10^26 operations. On May 13, 2025, the new administration rescinded it, promising in the same breath to "pursue a bold, inclusive strategy… while keeping the technology out of the hands of our adversaries." The weights control died with the rule. No replacement has issued. As of this writing there is **no standing US control on AI model weights at all.** (A universal negative owes its method: the only weights control ever issued is the one whose rescission notice we read, and the replacement BIS promised has never appeared — absence of a rule we searched for, not absence of looking.)

The chip gates opened next. The April 2025 license requirement on Nvidia's H20 — disclosed as an approximately $5.5 billion charge — was reversed by later license grants. Then, on January 13, 2026, Commerce moved exports of the far more capable H200 and AMD's MI325X from presumption-of-denial to case-by-case review, conditioned on preserving capacity for US customers, purchaser compliance procedures, and independent third-party US testing. "Export controls should evolve with changes in technology," the announcement said, "while protecting national security." One trade outlet reports — single source, not fetched — that a 25% tariff on the same chips was announced one day after the greenlight.

Both directions deserve their steelman. Control advocates: each loosening compounds China's training capacity; H20-then-H200 is a one-way ratchet. The administration: locked-out customers simply buy Huawei, and case-by-case review retains leverage a blanket denial forfeits. Both are coherent. The empirical crux — whether Chinese frontier training actually binds on compute — is unresolved.

So the ledger for eighteen months of American artifact-gatekeeping reads: weights control rescinded, chip controls loosened twice, one emergency closure by letter — reversed inside three weeks.

## One rulebook against fifty

Meanwhile the most energetic federal gatekeeping project of 2026 is aimed not at models but at **states**.

Executive Order 14365 (December 11, 2025) directs the Justice Department to stand up an AI Litigation Task Force to "challenge State AI laws inconsistent with the policy set forth in section 2," directs Commerce to identify "onerous" state laws, directs the FTC to say when state mandates making models "alter their truthful outputs" are preempted as deception, and cuts states with disfavored AI laws off from federal broadband money. The March 2026 Framework says the quiet part in its own words: "States should not be permitted to regulate AI development, because it is an inherently interstate phenomenon with key foreign policy and national security implications" — while also insisting "Congress should not create any new federal rulemaking body to regulate AI."

Congress has twice declined to do what the Framework asks: the ten-year state-AI moratorium was stripped from the 2025 budget bill 99–1, and a second attempt was omitted from the FY2026 defense bill. The politics scramble every expected alignment — the president declaring "There must be only One Rulebook," Governor DeSantis calling the moratorium "a subsidy to Big Tech."

The fight is now in court — and already moving under its own feet. xAI sued Colorado over its AI Act in early April 2026; on April 24 the DOJ moved to intervene on xAI's side — the first federal intervention against a state AI law — and on April 27 the court suspended enforcement, on a joint motion from xAI and the Colorado attorney general, not over the state's objection. Colorado has since moved the target itself, replacing the AI Act's discrimination duty with a disclosure regime around July 1, per secondary reports. On July 7 the FTC's proposed policy statement was published in the Federal Register; its theory, per the early legal analyses of it (we could not fetch the statement's own text): steering a model's outputs to comply with state law may be Section 5 deception even when the steering is done precisely to comply with that law. Complying with one sovereign's law may be deception under another's.

Now, the fact that organizes the whole American story. After the diffusion rescission and the revocation of the Biden executive order, **the only compute thresholds binding on US frontier developers anywhere in American law are state ones** — a negative grounded in the two federal removals just named plus the absence of any replacement rule. California's and New York's 10^26-FLOP frontier definitions, applied to developers above $500 million in revenue (for New York, that revenue threshold arrived with January 2026 amendments we have not fetched — as signed in December, RAISE keyed on $100 million in compute cost; treat the New York figure as reported): published risk frameworks, transparency reports, incident reporting within fifteen days (twenty-four hours if imminent). The federal government dismantled its own thresholds, produced recommendations that bind no one — and built a litigation machine aimed at the only thresholds still standing.

Steelman the administration: AI development genuinely is interstate; fifty discordant regimes really would tax small developers hardest; and some state mandates — output-steering for bias mitigation — may genuinely conflict with accuracy (the FTC's theory is not frivolous). Steelman the states: Congress has refused preemption twice; that power belongs to Congress, not a task force; and the enacted laws already answered the small-developer objection by exempting everyone below their thresholds — the small developer being defended is hypothetical, the developers being relieved the largest on earth.

## Brussels and Beijing

Europe spent 2026 deferring almost everything except the part that matters. The Digital Omnibus — Council final adoption June 29, 2026 — pushed the AI Act's stand-alone high-risk obligations to December 2, 2027 and product-embedded ones to August 2, 2028. But the general-purpose-AI chapter, the one that actually gates frontier models, was untouched — "No change to date," in Gibson Dunn's May 27 summary of the then-pending text, and nothing in the June 29 final adoption altered that: in force since August 2025, fining powers arriving August 2, 2026. Europe loosened its grip on the broad machine and kept its grip on the frontier.

China is the one jurisdiction running a true registration-and-licensing gate: mandatory filing of generative AI services — 72 approved in the March–April 2026 window alone; 346 services filed as of March 2025, per state media (a state claim; the page would not fetch). Add the labeling regime — explicit labels plus embedded watermarks and metadata, in force since September 2025 per multiple consistent secondary sources — and China has the most complete conduct-and-provenance gate in operation anywhere. And note what it does **not** gate: its laboratories export open model weights freely, to the world.

## The sincere case for the gate

Say it without caricature. A published jailbreak against cyber-capable frontier models is not a hypothetical; the June letter answered a specific, real technique. A state that can never say "stop" to a model has pre-committed to whatever happens next; June proved the stop button exists and works, and the resolution — a hardened classifier, deeper government access before release — plausibly left the world safer than the week before. And the process complaint below has to live with an asymmetry it cannot see around: the government may lawfully know threat details it cannot publish, and from inside that knowledge, speed-over-process can be the responsible choice rather than the lawless one. California's regime, the most gate-like thing in American statute, is not prohibition at all: it is disclosure, incident reporting, whistleblower protection — the mildest instruments regulation owns.

## The sincere case against

Also without caricature. Process first: a Friday-evening letter on contested statutory ground shut down two of the most used models on earth — for every researcher, clinic, and firm relying on them, worldwide — with no notice, no hearing, no published legal analysis. Efficacy second, and this is the sharp edge: the gate closes only on the legible. PIIE drew the conclusion within a day of the reopening — ad-hoc kill switches on American closed models advertise the one alternative no letter can reach: open-weight models, increasingly Chinese, to which — once the weights are downloaded — no provider can shut off access. One reported figure — located, not fetched, so treat it as a claim — has Chinese open-weight models growing from under 2% of OpenRouter token traffic in late 2024 to a majority in 2026. If even directionally true, the June letter demonstrated, at American expense, why the future might not run through anything a letter can touch.

And survey the pro-control coalition honestly: a rescinded rule, a stalled bill with zero cosponsors, a commission report, and one emergency power demonstrated only against the industry's most cooperative actor. As of July 2026, **nobody with enacted authority is controlling open weights anywhere.** Official US policy encourages open release — the July 2025 AI Action Plan, which we read in the original, calls open models "essential for academic research" and directs government to create "a supportive environment" for them. The EU regulates conduct with open-source carve-outs. China registers services, not weights. The most discussed gate in AI policy is a gate no one has built.

## Who holds the gate

The capture question has a naive version and a real one, and they point in different directions.

The naive version — safety rules as incumbent moats, compliance costs crushing the small — fails against the enacted texts: California and New York bind only the largest developers — California above $500 million in revenue, New York on the same figure as amended (as signed, $100 million in compute cost); the startup is exempt by construction. Whoever is being protected by the campaign against these laws, it is not the little guy.

The real version is about discretion. A case-by-case chip license with conditions converts a market into a queue with the government at the window; a tariff following a greenlight by one day (if the report holds) makes the gate a tollbooth. An "is informed" letter that can darken a model globally makes every frontier lab's continuity contingent on its standing with the Commerce Department — a dependency an incumbent can service with a government-affairs office and a newcomer cannot. Our hypothesis, tagged as one: **the deeper capture is not that rules burden the small, but that discretion binds everyone to the gatekeeper** — the more the regime runs on letters and case-by-case review instead of statute, the more it selects for the well-connected. The test, as data accumulates: compare licensing outcomes and informal-directive incidence for incumbents versus new entrants.

And notice, without conspiracy, who benefits structurally from June's precedent: the actors it cannot reach — open-weight releasers. A gate that closes only on your most legible, most compliant firms is a strange instrument of national advantage.

## Gatekeeping the deed, not the mind

Every gate in this essay is a gate around artifacts and access: chips, weights, APIs, market entry. Almost none is a gate around **proof** — a verifiable record of what a model actually did.

Yet look how the June standoff actually ended: not with the gate staying shut, not with artifacts changing hands — with the government able to *see more*. Read the full consideration in the exit, as The Record reports it: Anthropic agreed to "proactively detect and address security risks associated with the models," to "work with the government on standards for upcoming models," to inform the government of "malicious activity," and to give government evaluators expanded pre-release access. Every term is testimony — a promise to watch, to tell, to show. Not one is an artifact restriction; the sole artifact residue, Mythos's vetted-organizations gate, is transitional. The most dramatic artifact gate ever closed around a frontier model was reopened entirely for witness. The settlement was witness-shaped, not gate-shaped. And witness-layer law already exists, in fragments: California's incident-reporting clock. China's embedded watermark-and-metadata mandate — the most witness-shaped rule in force anywhere, enacted by the government least likely to be congratulated for it. Europe's synthetic-content transparency, due December 2, 2026 — even as its deepest witness rules, the high-risk logging obligations, were deferred to the end of 2027 and beyond.

The pattern we think we see — a hypothesis, test named, with its counterevidence stated inside it — is this. Artifact gates are brittle: the weights control rescinded, the chip controls reversed, the model shutdown lifted in under three weeks; against weights already downloaded, no gate exists even in principle. Proof gates would be cumulative: a record, once required and kept, survives the administration that required it. *Would* — the conditional is load-bearing, because the strongest counterexample sits in the paragraph above: the EU just deferred its deepest witness rules, the high-risk logging obligations, before they ever took effect. Proof gates evidently flip too, at least before they start; no enacted record regime has yet run long enough anywhere to show one surviving a hostile administration. And our brittleness evidence is mostly one country under one deregulatory administration — China's artifact gates have not flipped. So read the asymmetry as a conjecture about the shape of two instruments, not an observed law. The conjecture, stated: a polity that cannot decide whether to control minds could still decide to control **testimony about deeds** — non-repudiable logs, provenance marks, incident records, attestations of what ran where and did what. That is the road less discussed, and our stake must be disclosed: this publication emerges from a research program working on measurement and verification layers for AI systems — of course we find this road attractive. Discount accordingly. The test survives the bias: by the end of 2027, does any jurisdiction operate a verifiable record regime for frontier-model actions — the deferred EU logging deadline is the natural clock — or only more artifact gates, opened and closed by letter?

We titled this essay "the gates around the minds" and have said nothing about whether these systems are minds. That question stays where our editorial law keeps all such questions: not asserted, not denied, not abandoned — held open, while the work is done. But note the asymmetry the title hides. What a model *is* may be undecidable from outside for a long time. What a model *did* is decidable now, if anyone builds the witness. The governments of 2026 are arguing about the doors. Almost no one is keeping the ledger.

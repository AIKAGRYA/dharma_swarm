# Who Holds the Keys?

The debate over frontier-model access is often staged as safety versus
openness. That framing hides the harder problem. Some gates restrain dangerous
capability. Others determine who may research, build, compete, and know.
Frequently the same institution controls both.

Two fires are burning: increasingly capable systems may accelerate cyber,
biological, manipulative, or autonomous operations; and a small group of model
developers and cloud providers may accumulate epistemic and economic power.
Extinguishing either fire by feeding the other is not governance.

## The safety case is real

**FACT:** Frontier developers increasingly report capability-triggered
controls. OpenAI restricts some scaled agentic vulnerability research and
exploit chaining to an identity-gated cyber programme
([cybersecurity checks](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/safety-checks/cybersecurity)).
Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework uses capability levels across
CBRN, cyber, harmful manipulation, model R&D, and misalignment
([framework update](https://deepmind.google/blog/strengthening-our-frontier-safety-framework/)).
Anthropic ties stronger safeguards, security, review, and reporting to
capability assessments
([Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy)).

The UK AI Security Institute says high-fidelity evaluation may require
pre-release checkpoints, safeguards that can be disabled, helpful-only
versions, and fine-tuning access
([early lessons](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/early-lessons-from-evaluating-frontier-ai-systems)).
A public chatbot refusal is not equivalent to containment. Weight access,
tools, persistence, and scale change the threat model.

**HYPOTHESIS:** As models become more agentic, access design matters as much as
answer filtering. This would weaken if evaluations found no meaningful uplift
over existing tools or restrictions merely displaced misuse.

## The power case is also real

A gate appoints a gatekeeper.

OpenAI distinguishes verified users who may receive more permissive cyber
capabilities and publishes an appeal route
([transparency and moderation](https://openai.com/transparency-and-content-moderation/)).
Anthropic reports aggregate enforcement and appeals
([System Trust Reporting](https://www.anthropic.com/transparency/system-trust-reporting)).
These are better than invisible enforcement. They do not settle who allocates
access to essential capability.

**HYPOTHESIS:** Provider trust decisions shape which vulnerabilities are
found, which scientific questions are affordable, which languages receive
support, and which firms can build on the strongest systems. Geography,
pricing, contracts, and slow review can create epistemic hierarchy without an
explicit ban.

Economic concentration deepens the concern. A US Federal Trade Commission
staff report on Microsoft–OpenAI, Amazon–Anthropic, and Google–Anthropic
identified cloud-spending commitments, switching costs, access to sensitive
information, and possible constraints on compute and talent. It was a market
study, not a finding of illegality
([FTC report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/ftc-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-6b-study)).

The UK Competition and Markets Authority found infrastructure-as-a-service
highly concentrated and described Microsoft, AWS, and Google as vertically
integrated providers of accelerated compute and AI services
([CMA final report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/688b8891fdde2b8f73469544/final_decision_report.pdf)).

Safety requirements can become an incumbent advantage without bad faith. A
large provider can absorb monitoring, secure clusters, identity systems, and
regulatory reporting more easily than a university or entrant.

## Both fires: a test for legitimate gates

The choice is not release everything or trust the lab. A legitimate gate is:

1. **Hazard-specific:** it names the capability, threat, and pathway.
2. **Proportionate:** it restricts the dangerous affordance while preserving
   lower-risk use.
3. **Evidence-triggered:** escalation and removal thresholds are inspectable
   where disclosure does not itself enable harm.
4. **Independently testable:** qualified evaluators receive sufficient access.
5. **Contestable:** affected users receive reasons, appeal, consequential human
   review, and aggregate error reporting.
6. **Commercially neutral:** approval is not conditioned on buying the
   gatekeeper's cloud or distribution stack.
7. **Revisable:** controls have review dates and weaken when evidence no longer
   supports them.

The EU AI Act requires providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk
to evaluate and mitigate risks, document serious incidents, and maintain
cybersecurity
([European Commission Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/general-purpose-ai-models-ai-act-questions-answers)).
Its information-access procedure can make provider information available to a
scientific panel subject to proportionality and trade-secret safeguards
([Implementing Regulation 2025/454](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2025/454)).

**HYPOTHESIS:** Public authority reduces private sovereignty only when it has
technical competence, secure access, enforceable powers, and insulation from
industry capture and political censorship. A regulator that ratifies
provider-authored safety cases adds ceremony, not accountability.

## The institutional alternative

A defensible settlement is plural: multiple evaluators, reciprocal access
standards, shared secure infrastructure, provider portability, published gate
error rates, and appeals outside the original commercial chain.

**WILD:** Frontier access could become a credential commons rather than a
company loyalty programme. Portable, revocable capability credentials might
be earned through independent bodies. This could fail through capture,
credential laundering, international incompatibility, or the creation of a
privileged technical caste.

## What would change this

This analysis would change if independent evaluations found no meaningful
uplift in the named risk domains; enforcement data showed negligible false
positives and equal access among comparable users; market evidence showed low
switching costs; or regulators repeatedly demonstrated adversarial tests that
changed deployment decisions.

## Open questions

- **EMPIRICAL:** How much harmful capability remains after identity controls,
  monitoring, and tool restrictions?
- **DISTRIBUTIVE:** Which regions, languages, institutions, and firm sizes are
  disproportionately denied access?
- **INSTITUTIONAL:** Who can overrule a provider's safety classification?
- **TECHNICAL:** What minimum access distinguishes capability from safeguards?
- **ECONOMIC:** Do trusted programmes increase dependence on a provider's cloud?
- **LEGAL:** What due process applies when model access becomes infrastructure?
- **BUILD:** Prototype portable evaluator credentials with a threat model for
  capture, laundering, revocation, and cross-border recognition.

## 2. His core messages (faithful — what *he* says)

1. **There are two planetary crises, not one.** Climate gets the attention and the
   finance; the collapse of living systems — biodiversity and ecosystems — is the
   neglected "other planetary crisis." (McKinsey Author Talks; book.)
2. **Nature is an asset, and treating it as worthless is the root error.** Nature
   is *priceless*, but pricelessness has been allowed to mean *value-less* in
   economic decisions. **Natural-capital accounting** — putting nature on national
   and corporate balance sheets — makes its value legible so economies work *with*
   the living planet.
3. **Finance restoration, including via nature markets ("biocredits").** He
   profiles real pioneers and treats markets as one tool to fund *and monitor*
   regeneration.
4. **Credibility is the whole game.** In his peer-reviewed work he is explicitly
   *rigor-first*: existing nature markets are riddled with integrity failures, and
   credits should be issued **only after outcomes are demonstrated against an
   unbiased counterfactual**, with non-additionality assumed by default.
   (*Five rules for scientifically-credible nature markets*, Nature Ecology &
   Evolution, 2026, vol 10(2):181–192, with zu Ermgassen, Swinfield, Bull et al.;
   and *Nature-based credit markets at a crossroads*, Nature Sustainability, 2024.)
5. **Solutions are concrete and place-based** — Kanha's sal forests, Mumbai's
   mangroves, Palau's reefs, Indonesian jungles, Kenyan grasslands; plus
   ecotourism, rewilding, urban nature, and "Tech × Nature."
6. **Economics is necessary but not sufficient; worldview matters.** He closes on
   "the Indigenous case for nature" — nature must be re-woven into society and
   culture, not merely commodified — pre-empting the commodification critique.
